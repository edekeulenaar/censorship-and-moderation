# Part V. Modulation and consensus

What, then, can be done when enforcement can exclude speech from a service without settling the disagreement from which it arose? 

Part IV showed that deplatforming may reduce the visibility of a claim on one platform while giving its users, contents and grievances a reason to reorganise elsewhere. The question carried into Part V is therefore not how the same boundaries might be enforced more efficiently. It is how moderation might keep disagreement within a common space long enough for users to contest, revise and perhaps recognise the norm being enforced, instead of allowing every failed encounter to harden into rupture. If enforcement alone cannot secure cultural change, what other ways have platforms found of governing changing thresholds of tolerance, and can any of them provide a basis for metamoderation?

One response in the literature is to give states and their legislative functions greater authority over platforms. The argument begins with what Klonick calls the platform's role as a "new governor": companies make rules for public expression and administer them through private bureaucracies, but lack the representative mandate through which comparable exercises of public power are ordinarily legitimated [@klonickNewGovernorsPeople2017; @gorwaWhatPlatformGovernance2019b]. De Gregorio therefore argues that the protection of free speech is insufficient when the circulation of speech is organised through opaque technical standards and commercial priorities; platforms must also be subject to rights of explanation and to duties of transparency and accountability [@degregorioDemocratisingOnlineContent2020]. In these accounts, representative legitimacy gives legislatures a basis for setting the obligations and limits under which private moderation operates, while courts and administrative bodies supply review. The scholarly case is therefore for placing platform power under public and constitutional restraint, not for replacing the judgement of a company with the unanswerable judgement of a state.

Another answer proposed by the literature is that platforms have largely returned to a free speech absolutist philosophy. Watt, Montaña-Niño and Riedlinger interpret Meta's abandonment of third-party fact-checking as a shift towards political deal-making, while Cazzamatta finds that the accompanying language of censorship misrepresents a system in which removal was relatively rare and fact-checkers themselves generally preferred reducing the reach of false claims to deleting them [@wattArtPlatformDeal2025; @cazzamattaModerationChaosMetas2026]. Leerssen places the wider turn at Meta and X within the political economy of ownership: moderation can change with the interests, alliances and ideological commitments of the individuals who control the firm, even where these run against its previous institutional routines or commercial interests [@leerssenMurdochMuskPlatform2025]. Likewise, Magalhães, Iglesias Keller and Gorwa make the argument that X exemplifies *platform illiberalism*: the simplification of its governance institutions, the centralisation of authority in Musk and the repurposing of moderation mechanisms around his personal ideology [@magalhaesGreatSysopElon]. On this reading, platforms have weakened professional and plural sources of restraint while retaining the ability to intervene selectively. 

The chapters in this part approach the question through the changing techniques of moderation itself. Platforms have changed their strategies more than once. They have adapted to different, and often contradictory, demands from their users. Over time, this has meant moving away from the adjudication of individual pieces of content as simply acceptable or unacceptable and towards techniques that can accommodate changing thresholds of tolerance. Part V follows this movement on a single platform across two regimes: Twitter between 2006 and 2022, and its subsequent mutation into X.

Methodologically, the two chapters make moderation comparable across those regimes without assuming that Twitter and X expose the same data or use the same vocabulary. They reconstruct policy revisions and enforcement categories over time, then relate these to observable demotion and to the public Community Notes data through which agreement between otherwise opposed raters becomes measurable. The innovation is to study not only which speech is sanctioned, but how the decision procedure itself is reorganised: first by modulating visibility against a moving threshold, then by making cross-perspective agreement a condition of contextualisation. This provides an empirical answer to the fifth sub-question — how might moderation remain responsive to contestation without collapsing into either arbitrary enforcement or fragmentation? — while also exposing the limits of consensus systems whose terms remain platform-designed.

The first movement is towards modulation. [Chapter 13.  Modulating moderation](#/chapter-13) shows that Twitter increasingly relied on demotion as a comparatively agnostic way of moderating speech. Rather than requiring one fixed definition of objectionability, demotion can be adjusted to what users at different moments perceive as problematic. João Vieira Magalhães, Bharath Ganesh and I call this *modulated moderation*: an (aspiringly) crisis-resistant speech architecture designed to adapt to evolving and contingent demands from users and other stakeholders, particularly through normatively agnostic and dynamic moderation techniques. This "normative plasticity", as we call it, lies in its capacity to moderate according to evolving public conceptions of objectionability rather than to settle whether a piece of content is acceptable in any final sense. It can manage visibility against a moving threshold of tolerance.

The second movement is towards consensus-building. [Chapter 14. From Twitter to X](#/chapter-14) examines how Community Notes makes agreement between raters the engine of a moderation decision. The platform does not have to adjudicate a disputed claim directly but seeks to afford a process through which users who usually disagree might converge around a moderation decision, i.e., a note. This helps displace arbitrary normative decisions to the sum of agreements between users themselves. Though, of course, X has been far from exempt from accusations of partiality, the model of moderation proposed here brings several benefits to platform businesses: it sells itself as functioning on consensus-building as the engine of moderation. 

This is indeed an apparent return to moderation’s dialogical function, now delegated to an architecture and to the crowd. Its logic may also be extended beyond Community Notes: moderation decisions could be organised around the consensus that a system affords rather than around a judgement made by the platform alone. But consensus over a decision is not the same as consensus over the terms on which that decision is discussed. The problem of metamoderation remains: if users are to decide together how speech should be moderated, they must also be able to agree on the terms of their debate.

Read together, the two chapters describe moderation moving back towards the dialogical, consensus-building function with which this thesis began: the work of holding a plurivocal exchange together, now delegated to architecture and to users. They also leave open whether this movement genuinely recovers that function or only gives its appearance by relocating the labour of adjudication. More fundamentally, they leave unresolved how users might agree not only on particular moderation decisions but on the terms through which those decisions are debated. The Conclusion takes up these questions.
