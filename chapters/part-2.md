# Part II. Speech affordances

Part I proposed that moderation is best understood as a speech management mechanism designed for plurivocal environments and organised around two core functions. One is *enframing and enforcement*: establishing a threshold of tolerance and using modular, dynamic and minimally interventionist techniques to keep speech within it—labelling, obfuscating, demoting or otherwise marginalising objectionable speech before removal becomes necessary. The other is *facilitation*: maintaining socio-informational coherence, or the possibility for participants to understand one another, through contextualisation, mediation, redirection, fact-checking, balancing and summarisation. Together, these functions give moderation a constitutive role. They materialise the boundaries of permissible speech as policies, enforcement techniques, interface functions and user cultures. Users come to know what a platform is largely by learning what it will bear: which registers pass unremarked, which attract a flag, which are quietly buried, and which cross the final policy line. The studies gathered in this part therefore proceed from the claim that platforms and other information technologies are constituted, materially, by moderation rather than merely subjected to it.

This is less surprising once one recalls that most information and communication technologies are, at bottom, designed for the production and circulation of speech. What they offer their users are *affordances*: material possibilities for expression [@bucherAffordancesSocialMedia2018]. The philosophy of language has long made a version of this point without reference to platforms. Ayala describes one's "speech capacity" as dependent on the position one speaks *from* as much as the position of the interlocutor one speaks *to*, such that speech relies on "the detection and exploitation" of affordances embedded within a social structure and the norms that govern it [@ayalaSpeechAffordancesStructural2016, 1-4]. I call *speech affordances* the content moderation policies, techniques and user speech cultures that together signal what may and may not be said in a given space, and under what conditions — and that thereby govern how content circulates within and across more or less moderated regions of the Web.

Methodologically, this part makes a speech affordance observable by comparing what happens to the same object as it moves between normative environments. It combines cross-platform trace mapping of a meme, semantic comparison of replacement narratives across fringe and mainstream spaces, and *LLM perspectivism*: multilingual repeated prompting that introduces questions from Reddit's normative environment into eight language models, then compares how those models position themselves towards the same controversial speech. The innovation is to infer a norm not only from a written policy, but from transformations in wording, visibility and response: what an environment permits a speaker or model to say, and the rhetorical work required to remain sayable elsewhere. This answers the thesis's second sub-question — how are speech norms materialised across platforms and models? — while extending affordance analysis beyond interface features and platform-bounded policy audits.

[Chapter 3. The affordances of extreme speech](#/chapter-3) departs from a long-standing observation in linguistics and censorship studies — that speech moderation "materializes everywhere" as a practice that constitutes and defines the boundaries of publicly permissible language [@postCensorshipSilencingPractices1998]. On platforms, there are, first, the speech conventions and policies that determine the wider boundaries of expression: community guidelines, terms of service, and the local and international legislation and general zeitgeist they are modelled after. These are also "high-level" affordances in the sense that they "enable or constrain [...] communicative habits" [@bucherAffordancesSocialMedia2018, 12] without specifying, in themselves, how any single utterance will be handled. There are then the operationalisations of those conventions in the form of enforcement techniques: demotion, deplatforming, flagging, labelling, user reporting, strike systems and conditional penalties, along with the platform features that carry them. And there are the speech cultures of users themselves, which enforce norms of their own and occasionally against the platform's. 

Traced through the short life of the Kekistan meme, its immediate implication is that sanctioned language does not disappear so much as reconstitute itself across a wider geography of the Web. On 4chan/pol it circulates as an imaginary white ethno-state, a covert dog whistle intelligible to an in-group and not intended to travel. On Twitter, under the moral oversight of a wider and more ideologically diverse user base, it sheds its white nationalist subtext and mutates into a joke that imparts one message to its target audience and another to out-groups, and whose very ambiguity makes both user- and platform-led moderation reticent. In the more permissive YouTube comment section it appears in both versions at once. The meme's essential ambiguity, in other words, is not merely a rhetorical ruse but the consequence of a fragmentation of its meanings across platforms with distinct speech affordances.

Removed speech requires multiple linguistic subterfuges and displacements — a "necessary dissimulation" [@holquistIntroductionCorruptOriginals1994] that does not *say* what made it be silenced but signifies it by means of opacity. What these chapters add is that the displacement is at once linguistic and material: "extreme speech" is dissimulated in its wording and displaced across public spheres, transformed each time it passes from one affordance regime to another. [Chapter 4. The affordances of replacement narratives](#/chapter-4) follows one such displacement, namely how "replacement" narratives — the "great replacement" and "white genocide" codes — converge in the lesser moderated milieux of 4chan/pol and the YouTube comment section, and how they are transcoded into more mainstream political culture as they travel. The point is not that these milieux invent such speech, but that their affordances let it be recombined and disseminated in forms that stay sayable elsewhere.

[Chapter 5. LLMs and the generation of moderate speech](#/chapter-5) carries the same question into large language models. Where the earlier chapters concern the speech platforms afford their users, this one looks at how moderation, or "moderate speech", is imbued into the speech the models themselves generate. Comparing eight LLMs across the most controversial questions drawn from 165 subreddits in six languages, it examines the discursive techniques — refusals, defusal, and other rhetorical strategies — through which they answer prompts that sit along the boundaries of acceptability. It finds that models tend to meet high-"risk" prompts with refusals or normative language, but answer the most controversial prompts in markedly more inconsistent tones, shifting between agnostic, diplomatic and academic registers, and at times between iterations of the same prompt. The distinction reflects a deeper tension in value alignment, between anticipation — mitigating a harm estimated in advance — and deliberation — negotiating a controversy that is live precisely because it resists such estimation. Read this way, LLMs belong to a much longer history of speech moderation, and become a new kind of participant in the ongoing negotiation of public speech norms.

The notion of speech affordances is useful for locating the dynamics of speech moderation beyond platform policy. Given that deplatformed or marginalised speech never entirely goes away — certainly not under the conditions of the current social media landscape — the question that remains is how this affects cultural and social transformation. The disappearance or "forgetting" [@ricoeurMemoireHistoireOubli2006a] of ideas deemed problematic or unsound is an act upon a collective past through the language available to a collective present. The question that follows, and that returns in [Part V. Modulation and consensus](#/part-5), is therefore not only how far these affordances constrain speech, but how far they may afford dialogue about such constraints and the norms that sustain them. We will explore this question in [Part III. Moderation crises](#/part-3).
