# Part I. What is moderation?

Thus far in its history, moderation has often been thought in relation to platforms. Externally, it is often described as what platforms do when they intervene in what their users say, on whose authority, within which legal frameworks, and to what extent they may be held responsible for what they leave standing [@klonickNewGovernorsPeople2017; @gorwaWhatPlatformGovernance2019b]. Internally, it is a question of institution — or, how a set of dispersed housekeeping tasks acquired a corporate nature as trust and safety departments, community guidelines, classifier thresholds, outsourced review labour and the transparency reports that account for it [@robertsScreenContentModeration2019; @gillespietarletonCustodiansInternet2018]. Between the two, moderation is most times a function they perform, well or badly, upon a stream of user-generated content. 

The difficulty with platform-based definitions is that it often means thinking moderation in platforms' own terms [@gillespieFactContentModeration2023]. Yet, the controversies that make moderation contentious are often greater than the actors now charged with it. One aspect of such controversies is historical. Political communities debate how language ought to serve the cause of societal and cultural reforms through neologisms intended to create or facilitate new social realities, or through the revision of terms no longer thought appropriate [@mcintoshLanguageTrumpEra2020; @greenLanguageLullabiesRussification1997]. Through language, a collective decides the terms of its relations and the terms of its dialogue; it decides, too, what may come to be said, and under what conditions a thing said may be heard as legitimate. These terms act as levers that filter, remove, marginalise, reorganise, contextualise and otherwise handle speech based on more or less agreed norms. 

Framed this way, debates over how to handle speech are not preliminaries to the question of moderation, but are the question arriving under other terms. Indeed, moderation may be recognised more generally as a means to manage speech or information according to normative criteria, within a longer history of normative information management techniques. There are, of course, techniques other than removal: platform policies speak of filters, contextualisation labels, flagging, demotion, quarantining and rate limits. But stepping outside platform terminology altogether, another technique that most readily comes to mind — now for controversial reasons — is censorship, at least in its perception as a suppressive mechanism [@rbadouardPlateformesNouveauxCenseurs]. Another is moderation as a form of debate management, which is indeed among the earliest definitions of moderators as "steering" rather than "driving" an exchange toward a fruitful end [@brochetEffectiveModerationComputer1986]. 

So what is moderation, actually? To recognise it outside platform-specific studies requires an  interdisciplinary literature that situates it among other mechanisms by which speech is or has been managed. In this part, [Chapter 1. Censorship and moderation](#/chapter-1) is an attempt at exploring this literature. Setting aside the normative opposition through which moderation and censorship are usually held apart, this chapter proposes a distant reading [@morettiDistantReading2013] of a corpus of 1,985 articles, books, preprints and theses on content moderation and on neighbouring speech governance practices gathered across five languages and read for four conceptual components: how each publication defines the practice ("what"), to whom it attributes it ("who"), how it justifies it ("why"), and through which techniques it is carried out ("how"). 

From the taxonomy that results comes a claim that organises most of the thesis: that moderation is a speech management mechanism specifically developed for and within plural information environments. Its core task is to *enframe* a debate within core rules, be those epistemic (e.g., the rules of scientific debate); normative (e.g., the rules of anti-discriminatory speech in parliamentary debate); stylistic, or other. It is also to *sustain* conversation. To do so, it must refrain from intervening *as* an actor in a debate and instead steer the circulation of ideas and information until they reach a certain threshold of tolerance. To do, moderation uses a collection of modular and dynamic techniques intended to marginalise, obfuscate, label and otherwise minimise potentially transgressive speech. Should this threshold be transgressed, that speech may be censored (in the sense of removed). 

To sustain the course of debate, moderation must also apply a range of techniques that sustain an exchange rather than restrict it. At its core, it must ensure a minimum of socio-informational coherence. The moderator prompts a conversation, contextualises and mediates opposing arguments, calls for outside expertise, reframes questions so that the parties are contesting the same claim, aligns definitions, sequences issues so that a debate is neither overloaded nor derailed, balances participation so that quieter interlocutors can be heard, and summarises an exchange as it develops so that it can continue [@edwardsModeratorEmergingDemocratic2002; @audranForumQuoiBon2005; @taggLeadershipStudentModeration1994]. 

Moderation, as such, was initially practiced in simpler online fora. Wikipedia remains a good example: editors must engage with each other and understand to a reasonable degree how and why they make the decisions they do. They must also agree to the terms of their own editing and moderation. In other cases, moderators did many of the tasks associated today to information sorting algorithms: they recommended content to a given conversation; contextualised posts with external content; updated a newsfeed based on the latest states of a debate, and so on [@edwardsModeratorEmergingDemocratic2002; @wojcikModerateursForumsDiscussion2007b; @audranForumQuoiBon2005]. 

As platforms grew in size and needed to moderate "in scale" [@gillespieContentModerationAI2020], some of the capacities initially reserved to moderators — to prompt, contextualise, mediate, redirect and otherwise modulate what is exchanged — has been relegated to information sorting mechanisms primarily designed with simpler epistemic categories like engagement or relevance. Moderation is then left to deal primarily with adjudication, i.e., sorting and post-processing user generated content as acceptable or unacceptable (or somewhere in their borderlines). When it is reduced to this function, it risks forfeiting its legitimacy and creating an imbalance that signals a deeper loss of the consensus-building conventions through which a public arrives at the norms of its own debate.

The question applies empirically as much as conceptually. When we study moderation, what objects do we study exactly? Reading the broader literature cited in [Chapter 1](#/chapter-1), one notices that methods drawn from censorship studies and from digital methods invite us to treat moderation as something more than the drafting of platform trust and safety policies, which remain the primary object of study in the field  [@katzenbachPlatformGovernanceArchives2023]. In [Chapter 2. After deplatforming](#/chapter-2), I look instead at an ensemble of moderation mechanisms as a "moderation stack" that interconnects policy drafting to the techniques through which policy is enforced, to how users react to, evade and reinterpret enforcement, and, at the far end, migrate to alternative platforms. We speak of "effects" because this is what speech management mechanisms do: they process speech in a variety of ways and are visible primarily as effects rather than visible functions. 

Interdisciplinary literatures illustrate the point well. Censorship scholars and literary editors have long sought the effects of an intervention in the displacement, correction, disappearance and other residues it leaves behind [@holquistIntroductionCorruptOriginals1994; @darntonCensorshipComparativePerspective1994; @postCensorshipSilencingPractices1998]. They, too, scraped through interfaces looking for traces of modification and its impact on a final product: notes left in the margin of a censored play, redactions, censor bureau policies, and so on. As such, the chapter proposes to make these modifications an object of study by assembling a repertoire of trace methods for reconstructing what we term the "scene of disappearance" of moderated content. This means charting the evolution of moderation policy through the Internet Archive; retaining removable content through what we call *dynamic archiving*; capturing overt traces such as flags and labels; reading the wording users deploy to skirt enforcement; and following content and users as they replatform on less moderated spaces.

This method has since been operationalised into a research tool, MCAT — the Moderation Capture and Analysis Toolkit — developed with Ivan Kisjes, Frank Anemaet and finally Matúš Solčány [@dekeulenaarMCATModerationCapture2024]. The idea behind the tool is to afford the modularity with which moderation techniques and data access regimes change: what can be captured on a given platform in a given year is a moving target, and an instrument for studying moderation has to be able to move with it. Given a YouTube, X, Facebook or whatever dataset obtained from most social media platforms, MCAT looks for traces of removal, flagging, demotion and other metadata found through a combination of platform APIs and web scraping. These are clandestine methods for a clandestine object of study, much like the empirical study of censorship has tended to be.
