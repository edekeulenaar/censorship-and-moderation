# Part IV. Ruptures

The literature on alternative platforms has tended to begin with the forms of *alternativity* they sustain. Some accounts describe alt-tech as a collection of subcultures organised around the promise of uncensored expression and a shared grievance against “politically correct” moderation [@zuckermanDeplatformingOurWay2021]. Others emphasise alternative communication affordances: private or encrypted sociality, different systems of visibility and more permissive conventions for speech [@rogersDeplatformingFollowingExtreme2020]. A third body of work examines alternative infrastructures, from interfaces and open-source software to cloud hosts, domain registrars and business models [@donovanNavigatingTechStack2019; @siaperaAltTechPublic2023]. Taken together, this literature shows how alternative spaces create and circulate different speech codes, markets, technical arrangements and forms of political organisation.

Part IV asks how such alternatives are created through the moderation crises described in [Part III. Moderation crises](#/part-3). In [Chapter 10. Platform ruptures and the formation of online counter spheres](#/chapter-10), it calls a *platform rupture* a situation when users, platforms or other governing actors can no longer converge around the same threshold of tolerance. Since these norms are essentially contested, disagreement is an ordinary condition of moderation [@delaatCoercionEmpowermentModeration2012; @fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990]. Rupture begins when the disagreement becomes deep enough that the parties no longer share the premises through which it might be settled [@fogelinLogicDeepDisagreements1985]. At that point, the disputed content becomes a building block from which an alternative space is made, where it begins by affording what the original platform would not. From there, the content may be reorganised into a broader speech culture, moderation philosophy or epistemic project.

It is known that the deplatforming of political actors from Facebook and Twitter helped supply early audiences and purposes for Gab, Parler, GETTR and Truth Social [@rogersDeplatformingFollowingExtreme2020; @oteroWeNotMarshal2025]. The same can be said of waves of removal from YouTube increasing the relevance of BitChute and Rumble, at least in specific communities. Users have also left 4chan for 8chan and later 8kun, carrying with them both content and a demand for different moderation standards. When Cloudflare would no longer provide services to these spaces, alternative companies such as Epik and DDoS-Guard supplied the infrastructural conditions for their return. In each case, a boundary enforced at one layer became a reason to create or strengthen an alternative at another.

The methodological contribution of this part is to follow rejected material beyond the event of removal. It combines cross-platform timelines, edit and fork histories, inventories of unavailable videos, and users' own accounts of suppression and migration to reconstruct how an enforcement trace becomes an organising resource elsewhere. Whereas impact studies often ask whether deplatforming reduces reach on the service that acts, this ecological approach asks how users, contents and normative grievances are redistributed among services and infrastructural layers. It answers the fourth sub-question — what do moderation crises produce when they become ruptures? — by making the counter-sphere, rather than the deleted item alone, the unit of analysis.

The chapter is a summary of a presentation given to a symposium organised by the University of Copenhagen's Microsociology of Online Deviance lab by Jakob Demant and Alessandro Moretti. It has not yet been published.

[Chapter 11. Epistemic ruptures in the encyclopedias of alternative facts](#/chapter-11) provides an example from the wiki space. It tells the story of how Wikipedia editors were removed from the platform after pushing for edits that alleged correlations between race and intelligence in an article about its historical controversies. They then forked a version of the article they preferred into another wiki: first, Metapedia, and then Infogalactic and Rightpedia. Each of these were the projects of contested edit histories and unresolved disagreements. What began as a contested entry became material for platforms of alternative facts, reproducing Wikipedia’s entries and claims to authoritative knowledge while changing the epistemic norms by which that knowledge was judged. The point of rupture — and the first building blocks of alternative wikis — was therefore both textual and normative: a version of an entry and an account of why it had been excluded.

The chapter is an early article co-authored with Marc Tuters, Ivan Kisjes and Kaspar Beelen. Marc and I wrote the article together, while Ivan and I designed the method, and Kaspar contributed to the word2vec analyses of wiki text. Together, we organised several DMI data sprints on the broader topic of alternative wikis.

[Chapter 12. Deplatforming, demotion and folk theories of Big Tech persecution](#/chapter-12) follows the same relation after YouTube’s moderation of COVID-19 conspiracy narratives. Between April and October 2020, YouTube combined “hard” moderation, through video and account removal, with “soft” moderation, through the demotion of borderline content and the promotion of authoritative sources. The chapter reconstructs these interventions and examines how users interpreted them. The policies were effective in a limited but important sense: thousands of videos became unavailable, and search and recommendation systems did reduce the visibility of claims about 5G, microchips, depopulation and other COVID-19 conspiracy narratives. But it did so at the cost of reorganising YouTube's role within a broader market of conspiracy theories. Users removed from YouTube would congregate on BitChute to pursue the theorisation of various conspiracies, while YouTube, to them, became a source of primary material for such theorisations posted by and for conspirators. Enforcement therefore reorganised the relation between platforms according to opposite but complementary normative criteria. 

The chapter is a journal article co-authored with Anthony Burton and Ivan Kisjes. Ivan designed the method, while Anthony and I designed the study and shared the writing.

Taken together, the chapters show that a platform which enforces a threshold of tolerance without providing a shared venue for negotiating it does not bring the underlying disagreement to an end. It can remove content from one service, but the speech, knowledge and users involved may reorganise across others. Rupture is thus the form this displacement takes as the creation of counter-spheres whose affordances are built around what the original platform would not permit, and whose continued use remains connected to it.

This is especially important when moderation is expected to contribute to cultural change — under the premise that the disappearance of language allows the normalisation of preferred alternatives. Cultural transformation depends on some degree of internalisation: the possibility that publics understand, contest and eventually recognise why a form of speech has become unacceptable [@haddenWhatsNormMapping2016, 250; @rossiVarietiesNormativeConsensus1985, 334]. Deplatforming without metamoderation may instead turn a rejection into a counter-argument to be extended in spaces that afford it. As a strategy for cultural change, enforcement alone thus displaces disagreement without necessarily transforming the norm from which the disagreement arose.

The three chapters that follow examine this movement at different levels: the alternative spaces that ruptures bring into being ([Chapter 10](#/chapter-10)); the alternative knowledge produced through epistemic rupture ([Chapter 11](#/chapter-11)); and the ways users reorganise, rather than end, their relationship with platforms that sanction them ([Chapter 12](#/chapter-12)). This leaves the question that motivates [Part V. Modulation and consensus](#/part-5). If enforcement is not sustainable on its own, what other forms of moderation can and have platforms been adopting?
