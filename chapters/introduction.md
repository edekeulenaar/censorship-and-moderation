# Introduction

One common denominator of Internet studies is an interest in how social media platforms reconfigure conditions for public debate. Depending, of course, on the geopolitical vantage point a study inadvertently adopts, many platforms were once celebrated for "liberalising" political expression from the editorial gatekeeping of legacy media. The Arab Spring was for a time an emblematic case study of how platforms would turn individual expression into a form of democratic participation [@howardDemocracysFourthWave2013]. Decades later, these same mechanisms would liberate "problematic" actors and ideas until then relatively marginalised in public debate [@mungerRightWingYouTubeSupply2022a], to think of political forces that straddled the norms of public expression in places long assumed to be mature democracies [@eatwellNationalPopulismRevolt2018].

One diagnosis of this transformation has tended to frame platforms as producing *excessive information*. On this account, the relaxation of editorial gatekeeping allows bad actors to re-emerge in public and at times corrupt public opinion through manipulation and coordinated influences, all while pushing the boundaries of platform policies into a sense epistemic and moral relativism. The implicit critique is that the often arbitrary and forbidding gatekeeping forces of the past at least ensured some basic standards of informational integrity and cohesion [@shoemakerGatekeepingTheory2009]. Their sudden dismantlement set the scene for a variety of "information disorders" [@socialscienceresearchcouncilHateSpeechInformation2020] where incommensurably diverse perspectives clash and produce a general atmosphere of toxicity, particularly as a form of misinformation and hostile language, however defined.

From a regulatory perspective, a good deal of responses have consisted in honing a more decisive answer to what counts as acceptable speech in the first place. Much has been said of EU and (as of late) Brazilian platform regulation as quests to recover a basic sense of normalcy to public expression, particularly by reinforcing editorial norms against discrimination, sedition and attempts to overthrow key institutions [@europeanunionRegulationEU20222022]. But with the level of public debate around such legislation, one can place these agreements within an otherwise constantly evolving and contentious timeline of debates on what is objectionable, how and to whom. One of the few constants to emerge from this timeline is that speech norms remain "in constant negotiation" [@fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990] over which aspects of history may or may not be allowed to recur through language and behaviour (see @chongCompetingNormsFree2018b). 

One of the contenders in this debate are platforms themselves. In response to the "massive pluralism" [@weylCommunityDesign2026a] of their user base, social media have somewhat inadvertently (Wynn-Williams, 2025) centralised the means to manage and define acceptable public speech [@sabloskyDangerousOrganizationsFacebooks2021], at least for a time. Distinct from federalised or community-based moderation systems [@rozenshteinModeratingFediverseContent2023], the majority of social media platforms do often detain the last word around the moderation of their own services. This has attracted accusations of bypassing a necessary negotiation with publics and elected speech governing actors; a step some have described as "platform authoritarianism" [@magalhaesGreatSysopElon2026; @rauchbergPlatformAuthoritarianismCreator2026]. More fundamental interrogations lie on the nature of platform power relative to that of whatever is deemed "traditional" in the wider history of public governance, to name states [@klonickNewGovernorsPeople2017; @badouardRegulationContenusInternet2020] and civil society [@balkinFreeSpeechTriangle2018].

But while platforms detain many of the means to speech governance, they have also redistributed governance affordances amongst the actors they host [@rbadouardPlateformesNouveauxCenseurs]. So emerges a strange paradox. Platforms may well have expanded the participative affordances of public expression, including the possibilities for communities to manage their own exchanges [@edwardsModeratorEmergingDemocratic2002]. But they have also become sites *and* actors of conflicts about the very terms of public debate. 

For one, censorial powers have been redistributed and to a certain extent democratised as a _private_ right [@cookTwoTypesSelfCensorship2013a; @messinaPrivateCensorship2023] granted to a variety of actors [@gyetvanCensorshipFreedomExpression2025; @amadieuFormesContemporainesCensure2020], who may themselves grant the right affordances for other groups to exercise censorial powers of their own. These are censorial affordances in a descriptive sense, as means to act upon the access, visibility and circulation of other speech, even when their purpose is to simply to sustain a community rather than repress opposing viewpoints. At an infrastructural level, we may find states, cloud service providers or app stores removing access to entire platforms. At the level of the service, trust and safety teams draft and enforce the terms under which everyone else speaks. Within and by way of platforms, we will find “more or less ephemeral associations […], groups or subgroups” [@amadieuFormesContemporainesCensure2020, 91] exercising pressure or “cancel” campaigns against another group, issue or cultural asset; and at the finest grain, individual users muting, blocking, reporting and deleting  [@meisnerWeaponizationPlatformGovernance2023; @jiangTradeoffcenteredFrameworkContent2023]. 

There are also fundamental disagreements on what constitutes objectionable information, and on the basis of which personal and collective memory [@chongCompetingNormsFree2018b]. The result is competing speech norms, each practicing a more or less exclusive understanding of what accounts as objectionable under different thresholds of tolerance. Controversies around identity and social justice, historical revisionism and the general limits of political expression often do not arrive in these spaces with the same historical  premises. Platforms themselves tend to align more or less with one or another premise, under their own thresholds of tolerance relative to their user groups and competing platforms. These thresholds are operationalised into a variety of filtering, demotion, ranking and summarisation techniques, often in direction confrontation with states, civil society organisations, advertisers and other interested actors seeking to orient policy according to norms of their own [@balkinFreeSpeechTriangle2018]. 

That this should be a problem at all may have come as something of a surprise to platforms as products of a profound sense of liberalism — projects to aggregate the greatest possible number of users into a single speech market. Within the history of the Web, they restate an older proposition that history, understood as a sequence of fundamental ideological confrontations, resolves itself through consensual deliberation as the main engine of liberal-democratic politics [@fukuyamaEndHistoryLast2006] — provided the tolerance extended to freedom of thought is generous enough. What was less anticipated is their limitation precisely at competing and oftentimes irreducibly different thresholds of tolerance, either with other governing actors, with their own users, and with their competitions. Here, the disagreement is not only about what may be said, but about how much may be borne.

Though these constitute freedoms in their own right, they also demonstrate the relative absence of consensus-building mechanisms for publics to coalesce around the terms of their own debate. Consensus-building as such is of course never finite [@ricoeurMemoireHistoireOubli2006a]. The question is at once descriptive of the difficulties moderation encounters and an invitation to consider *metamoderation*: the deliberation not of content but of the rules by which content is judged, and of whether those rules are being applied as agreed [@geigerBotbasedCollectiveBlocklists2016b; @seeringModeratorEngagementCommunity2019]. The question that arises is therefore less what platforms ought and ought not to tolerate — and what states ought and ought not to restrict through them — than how citizens and their public spheres can come to some agreement about the very terms of public debate in platformized public spheres. 

Throughout this thesis, I call *speech norms* these more or less explicit agreements. Unless they are decided and enforced by authority [@allanForbiddenWordsTaboo2006], they can be the result from explicit or implicit social, cultural and political processes that produce a common (but certainly variable) understanding of why we agree to speak to one another the way we do. Readers of the philosophy of communication may recognise the notion of "understanding" here as resembling Habermas' notion of the "lifeworld" of dialogue [@habermasContributionsDiscourseTheory2020, 14], i.e., the background information used to make sense of the norms of linguistic interaction. One may think of memorial and anti-discrimination laws as efforts to canonise historical lessons on "words that hurt" : speech is seen as conjuring, and thus re-legitimising circumstances from the past [@butlerExcitableSpeechPolitics1997] that the state [@levinsonSilencingPublicMonuments] or communities of people [@matsudaWordsThatWound2018] "never again" wish to be repeated. All around may lay monuments, stories, policies and political discourses to explain why as a form of remembrance, reconciliation or retributive justice [@ricoeurMemoireHistoireOubli2006a]. Statues may be overthrown and flags changed, an idea or public figure cancelled or redeemed, and in all cases new agreements are produced on what words mean and why and how they ought to be used in a particular way. 

These agreements also do a great deal. They can be prescriptive, in the sense that they participate in movements to reform culture, social relations (for example, between genders, ethnicities or other group denominations), or political processes, to think of decommunisation, derussification [@greenLanguageLullabiesRussification1997], denazification [@vincentPunirReeduquerProcess2008], or (re)democratisation processes foreclosing military regimes [@vizquadratHistoriaMemoriaPolitica2016]. A key pretext is to redirect and otherwise transform speech in ways conducive to historical change [@mcintoshLanguageTrumpEra2020], relative to the political prescriptions of whatever movement. Language becomes a vector of change by virtue of its power to designate what can and cannot be said on what is and can be. It also becomes a means to manage what is against what could be, as is the case with German anti-extremism laws acting as democratic "militancy" or "self-defence" [@loewensteinMilitantDemocracyFundamental1937]. Crystallized into legislation, institutional frameworks and political culture, these techniques constitute memorial levers by which individuals presently act upon their collective past. 

Speech norms also detain a constitutive force; they materialise materialise into "discursive architectures" [@mecheverriaArquitecturaDiscursivaPlataformas, 2] that "accommodate some expressive modes and not others” [@fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990]. These may be "legacy" media [@martinCensuresDansMonde2016], platforms of all sizes [@gillespietarletonCustodiansInternet2018] and kinds [@gorwaModeratingModelMarketplaces2024b], and more fundamentally language itself [@allanForbiddenWordsTaboo2006; @bourdieuLangagePouvoirSymbolique2001]. There, speech norms are operationalised into various affordances for expression [@bucherAffordancesSocialMedia2018]. There are affordances proper to censorious media regimes [@postCensorshipSilencingPractices1998], to the facilitation of public debates [@habermasNewStructuralTransformation2023], to editorial norms that "gatekeep" [@shoemakerGatekeepingTheory2009], fact-check and otherwise edit information; to media regulation; to AI alignment [@gabrielArtificialIntelligenceValues2020], and to any other environment that moulded into specific possibilities for expression. The limitations of these affordances will also create passages into new ones: there are nooks and crannies from which speech sneaks through wherever it is best expressed [@dezeeuwTracingNormieficationCrossplatform2020], camouflaging, adapting, or taking the opposite shape of its former restraints. 

For these environments to remain public, they require some means of building and maintaining agreement around the norms of expression themselves. Different actors and mechanisms have, over time, facilitated the production of such agreements about the meaning and use of words. They include political and legal institutions, editorial conventions, journalists, churches, schools, associations and the many informal authorities through which a public learns why certain expressions ought to be accepted, restrained or rejected. They also provide, however imperfectly, places in which the reasons for speaking in one way rather than another may be formulated, contested and eventually sedimented into common use. The process if of course never entirely achievable: at issue are norms that are *essentially* contested [@delaatCoercionEmpowermentModeration2012] and subject to deep disagreements [@fogelinLogicDeepDisagreements1985] that result in partial or no consensus at all. Consensus, here, should therefore not be confused with unanimity but with the more modest condition in which participants deliberate and arrive at relative understanding of the meanings, procedures and authorities of the norms by which they engage in public debate. 

Though moderation has long been accused as an aggravating factor in the production of this consensus [@myerswestCensoredSuspendedShadowbanned2018; @rbadouardPlateformesNouveauxCenseurs], it has never in its history been antithetical to it. In political and communicational philosophy, moderation is sometimes described as a condition and an ensemble of mechanisms that make dialogue possible [@grimmelmannVirtuesModeration2015]. A moderator is there to ensure an equal opportunity to participate in deliberation as a political ethos grounded in dialogue, mutual adjustment and compromise among opposing camps [@craiutuFacesModerationArt2017]. It is less an agreement on substantive ideas than a way of maintaining the conditions for dialogue. Early writings on *content* moderation spoke precisely of moderators ensuring the conditions for exchange and intelligibility in online fora [@edwardsModeratorEmergingDemocratic2002; @wrightDemocracyDeliberationDesign2007b], their work consisting primarily of framing discussions, aligning definitions, balancing participation, supplying context and preserving enough socio-informational coherence for conversations to take course.

This definition gained less favour than that of moderation as *enforcement*, as platforms were compelled to reinforce moderation "at scale" [@gillespieContentModerationAI2020] in response to major scandals, regulatory demands and a growing repertoire of objectionable content [@hillDefinitelyNotBusiness2024]. The functions of monitoring, detection, removal and marginalisation could be rendered into policies, classifiers and industrial workflows, while the more dialogical work of moderation as *debate management* was partly displaced into algorithmic systems favouring simpler epistemic categories, such as relevance, popularity and freshness. 

The underlying question to move this study, then, is less about proposing a framework for defining and defending a notion of objectionability then about understanding this larger reconfiguration of the logic of moderation. I explore five of its aspects: how speech norms have formed platform content moderation practices; how the struggle to form consensus around such norms affects the wider geography of online public spheres; and how content moderation strategies evolve in relation to various crises of consensus and legitimacy. 

In [Part I. What is moderation?](#/part-1), I ask: what kind of speech governance technique is moderation in the context of other techniques, such as censorship, gatekeeping, or debate management? How does the literature theorise speech moderation beyond new media studies, and what light do such definitions bring about how else speech moderation has been practiced in history, or otherwise today? 

Given its theoretical background, how does one set out to study moderation practices? How does one capture moderation when, like censorial practices, it tends to only leave *traces* behind rather than explicit interventions? 

In [Part II. Speech affordances](#/part-2), the guiding question is: how does moderation come to constitute platforms and platform speech cultures? How does it shape the circulation of more or less acceptable information across platforms and platform spaces (for example, commenting spheres)? 

In [Part III. Moderation crises](#/part-3), I ask: how does moderation become subject to disagreements across users, platforms, different media and institutions? What happens when such actors disagree about, or fail to share, the basic definitions of categories of unacceptable speech? 

In [Part IV. Ruptures](#/part-4), I then ask: what are the consequences of such disagreements in creating counter spheres, including alt-tech platforms? What is the long-term impact of this process of platform fragmentation? 

Finally, in [Part V. Modulation and consensus](#/part-5), I explore how such disagreements have transformed content moderation techniques and broader strategies, be it demotion or including Community Notes.

Each of these parts and their chapters are detailed below. 

##### Part I. What is moderation?

[Part I. What is moderation?](#/part-1) establishes the conceptual and methodological ground for the thesis. 

[Chapter 1. Censorship and moderation](#/chapter-1) begins from a confusion its object has come to embody — that "moderation" and "censorship" are now used almost interchangeably by platforms and their critics alike — and asks what kind of practice moderation is once that normative opposition is set aside. Reading content moderation studies alongside the literature on adjacent speech governance techniques, especially censorship, it proceeds through a distant reading [@morettiDistantReading2013] of a large corpus of scholarship on speech governance, from which I build a taxonomy of how moderation and censorship are defined, by whom they are practiced, why, and through which techniques. 

[Chapter 2. After deplatforming](#/chapter-2) then asks how moderation can be studied empirically, developing a framework for reconstructing the "scene of disappearance" of moderated content, later extending the concern to the moderation behaviour of LLMs in [Chapter 5. LLMs and the generation of moderate speech](#/chapter-5). Having mentioned various works from censorship studies in the previous chapter, it draws from the methods of censorship scholars. I focus on five components of moderation: its documentation in the form of platform policies; how such documents can be used to determine what is moderated and how; reverse-engineering various moderation practices, such as removal, flagging or demotion; and exploring how users react to such effects.

##### Part II. Speech affordances

[Part II. Speech affordances](#/part-2) examines how speech norms come to be materialised in the technical and (sub)cultural fabric of platforms. By "speech affordances", I mean the policies, techniques, and user speech cultures that together signal what may and may not be said in a given space and thereby govern how content circulates within and across more- and less-moderated regions of the Web. 

[Chapter 3. The affordances of extreme speech](#/chapter-3) theorises this through the affordances of extreme speech, following the migration of a meme across 4chan, Twitter, and YouTube. Such affordances materialize what may and may not be said in a given platform (and how), and thereby forms the relation and information circulation across different spaces (for example, from “mainstream” to “alt-tech” spaces). 

In turn, [Chapter 4. The affordances of replacement narratives](#/chapter-4) develops a case study of "replacement" narratives, attending to the linguistic subterfuges — displacements, dissimulations, coded "transcodings" — through which a taboo is signalled without being stated. Both describe how speech norms materialize into speech affordances and form counter-spheres with competing affordances.

[Chapter 5. LLMs and the generation of moderate speech](#/chapter-5) explores how speech norms are operationalised in LLM text generation. It is about how five eight models (GPT 3.5 and 4, Llama2 and 3, Claude Sonnet, Haiku, and Mistral) respond to the top controversial questions posted on multilingual Q&A subreddits. I explore the discursive techniques each LLM uses — or what I call “moderate speech” — when responding to questions with varying degrees of controversy and risk, as defined by each model's moderation API. 

##### Part III. Moderation crises

Having explained how speech norms become a formative component of platforms as public spheres, in [Part III. Moderation crises](#/part-3) I describe what happens when such norms diverge across (1) platforms and their users; (2) platforms and legal institutions; and (3) platforms, users and media organizations. The main focus on this chapter is to describe how dissensus emerges across these actors with regards to what they consider to be acceptable or truthful, and eventually what the impact of such dissensus is on the formation and use of "alt-tech" as examples of online counter-spheres.

[Chapter 6. Moderation in crisis](#/chapter-6) examines conflict between platforms and their publics through the case of YouTube comments between 2004 and 2022. The study is about how YouTube commenters define and dispute what is hateful, misinformation and otherwise unacceptable in the platform throughout 2004 and 2018, and how eventual disagreements shape the way users view the platform from an “open sphere” to a corporate entity. 

[Chapter 7. Of migrants and strangers](#/chapter-7) turns to the dissensus between platforms and the editorial norms of *legacy media*, following how Dutch broadcasters and their social media audiences diverged over the "refugee crisis" of 2013–2018. Here, my co-authors and I explore how social media publics and Dutch broadcasters begin to diverge around Dutch speech norms about discrimination in relation to the crisis (2015-2018). As users and broadcasters frame the refugee crisis in different normative lenses, they also began to produce mutually alternative facts about the crisis.

[Chapter 8. Twitter as an accidental authority](#/chapter-8) considers platforms and public health organisations, showing how Twitter became an inadvertent or "accident" authority while trying to surface authoritative information about COVID-19 transmission, treatment and prevention information through its fact-checking systems. As local health organizations and state authorities failed to maintain stable consensus over time and amongst themselves, Twitter steps in as an authority in its own right by moderating those public health authorities for allegedly spreading falsehoods. 

Finally, [chapter 9](#/chapter-9) addresses the dissensus between platforms and broader processes of transitional justice. It introduces the concept of *normative dislocation* to explain how US-rooted moderation standards failed to register local Brazilian prohibitions on militaristic speech during the 2022 elections by prioritising electoral "misinformation" over open calls for a military coup. It shows how this “dislocation” allowed the spread of content once contained by legacy media, and threatened to maintain a fragile but key historical consensus against military political violence.

##### Part IV. Ruptures

[Part IV. Ruptures](#/part-4) considers what emerges from deep disagreements about content moderation norms. 

[Chapter 10. Platform ruptures and the formation of online counter spheres](#/chapter-10) defines “alt-tech” platforms as counter-spheres that emerge from unresolved disagreements about speech norms. It charts a brief history of a few major normative ruptures that have seen the constitution of multiple alt-tech spheres across the Web, namely cloud hosts,  blogging sites, wikis, social media and messaging applications. It proposes the notion of "platform ruptures" to describe a complementary trend to "platformisation" [@helmondPlatformizationWebMaking2015].

[Chapter 11. Epistemic ruptures in the encyclopedias of alternative facts](#/chapter-11) turns from technical and platform ruptures to epistemic ones, following how editors banned from Wikipedia fork its content into rival encyclopaedias like Metapedia, Infogalactic and Rightpedia — Grokipedia did not exist just yet. Reading Wikipedia through Lyotard's notion of postmodern conditions for knowledge production, it argues that the platform's consensus-and-neutrality ideal meets a limit wherever disagreements prove irreconcilable. Alternative wikis arise at those fault lines not as a symptom of "post-truth" indifference to facts, but as attempts to give alternative epistemic viewpoints a platformed, and thus "universal", format.

[Chapter 12. Deplatforming, demotion and folk theories of Big Tech persecution](#/chapter-12) examines the obverse dynamic — that is, how deplatforming and demotion become entangled with the conspiratorial "folk theories" users develop about moderation itself during YouTube's handling of COVID-19 "borderline content". It is placed under this section because it illustrates how the users YouTube deplatforms do not stop using the platform, but simply change their uses in a way coherent with the narratives they were deplatformed for. Users sanctioned for COVID-19 conspiracy theories may post their original content on Bitchute but still use YouTube as a primary source of study — i.e., the place where conspirators dwell, including YouTube itself. 

#### Part V. Modulation and consensus

[Part V. Modulation and consensus](#/part-5) looks at how platforms dealt with the crises of consensus and legitimacy addressed above.

[Chapter 13.  Modulating moderation](#/chapter-13) explores how objectionable content has been defined by Twitter content moderation practices from 2010 to shortly after Musk’s takeover. It argues that Twitter has transitioned from an adjudicative to a modular form of content moderation, which uses dynamic techniques, such as demotion, to build what my co-authors and I call a "crisis-resistant" speech architecture that is largely normative agnostic. 

The final study, [Chapter 14. From Twitter to X](#/chapter-14), is about how X, in turn, extended Twitter’s policy of modularity to “consensus-building mechanisms” such as Community Notes. Read against the broader call for "prosocial" alternatives to punitive moderation, it asks whether moderation is now drifting from adjudication back toward the dialogical, consensus-seeking function with which the thesis began — returning to some of the questions posed in [Chapter 1. Censorship and moderation](#/chapter-1).

#### Methodologies

Throughout, the thesis operationalises notions from political philosophy and other fields that have contributed to the study of speech governance into digital methods [@rogersDigitalMethods2013]. Because moderation is ephemeral and frequently invisible, capturing it requires a layered approach: *trace research* that reconstructs what has been removed, demoted, or relabelled from the residue it leaves behind; *dynamic archiving* that captures content before and as moderation acts upon it; distant reading for the comparative analysis of large textual corpora; natural-language-processing techniques such as word embeddings, word trees, and subject–verb–object networks for comparing how different publics frame the same events; and close, qualitative reading of platform policies, interface changes, and the conceptual literature. 

The visuals in this thesis pursue two aims that occasionally pull in opposite directions. The first is to give justice to express the complexity and richness of platform data, particularly when it involves controversy mapping. Controversies, especially around moderation, tend to resist tidy summaries, particularly when they involve fairly unstable actors and ideas over time, oftentimes more nuanced than sociological and political categorisations propose. This is why several chapters reach for forms built to carry distributional and relational nuance, such as alluvial (or Sankey) diagrams that trace relations between multi-categorical data; beeswarm plots that can show individual posts arrayed over time rather than collapsed into averages or categories; word trees and word-embedding maps that make visible how user discussions branch through contested terms. 

The second aim is to render legible the workings of moderation systems themselves. Moderation is, by design, a process that prefers to remain unseen. The thesis treats this invisibility as something to be remapped, or "reverse-engineered". A timeline of a platform's evolving COVID-19 policy makes a moving target hold still long enough to be examined; a beeswarm of flags and suspensions shows when, how often, and against what a platform intervene. In some cases, reconstructions take the form of an interactive web visualisation to allow a reader to traverse a full dataset for other potential conclusions outside of those made by a given a chapter. In this register, the visualisation serves as partial evidence of what remains after moderation.

Finally, many of the studies in this thesis received ethical reviews throughout peer-reviewed journal assessment, but did not go through internal university procedures. The reason why is because ethical concerns were often already embedded in the methods I designed or used from colleagues. 4CAT, for example — a tool I use in various occasions for data collection and analysis — anonymises all user data into hash values. In the occasions I collected data myself, the same method was applied. More generally, questions about user identity — in the sense of demographic, gender, personal or other data — were not part of my research questions. Of interest were textual or visual expressions of their ideas and norms, quanti and qualified into topical clusters. 

#### The writing process

This is an article-based thesis. It assembles a body of empirical and conceptual work produced from roughly 2019 (before the exact start of a PhD programme) to 2025. 

The intellectual trajectory tended to run from the particular to the general. It began with concrete studies on how normative or epistemic disagreements result in "platform ruptures" [@dekeulenaarAltpediasPartisanEpistemics2019]; how moderation is practiced (via deplatforming, demotion or other) and how it transforms specific platform uses (e.g., conspiracy theorisation) [@dekeulenaarDeplatformingDemotionFolk2021a]; how moderation systems constitute "speech affordances" [@dekeulenaarAffordancesExtremeSpeech2023] and mould the itineraries of deplatformed content across platforms [@dekeulenaarAffordancesReplacementNarratives2023]; how policy concepts are shaped [@dekeulenaarModulatingModerationHistory2023a]; how moderation intervened in public health confusions over COVID-19 [@keulenaar6TwitterAccidental2023]; how, after a series of public crises, emerging platforms changed their underlying moderation philosophies [@dekeulenaarTwitterDemotionCommunity2025]; and how speech norms are implemented in new information management systems like LLMs [@dekeulenaarLLMsGenerationModerate2025]. [Chapter 6. Moderation in crisis](#/chapter-6) is a study presented at the Association of Internet Researcher's Conference in 2022, and [Chapter 10. Platform ruptures and the formation of online counter spheres](#/chapter-10) a presentation given at a workshop at the University of Copenhagen in 2024. [Chapter 1. Censorship and moderation](#/chapter-1) is an updated version of a presentation given to a conference on censorship in 2023, in Lisbon, which later became a book chapter [@zotero-item-46680]. 

The conceptual apparatus presented in the opening pages is, in that sense, a retrospective synthesis: it names what the case studies, taken together, turned out to be about.

#### A note on bibliographic readings

Like many debates in new media studies, those on content moderation tend to have their centre of gravity in the United States. Though that might be a natural effect of the overbearing presence of US scholarship in communications and media studies, the result is a tendency to universalise a particular legal and cultural settlement around free expression, free speech controversies and protections of minorities as articulated by American notions of identity and demographic history. 

Though it might be positioned in anti-hegemonic intellectual traditions, this logic tends to be hegemonic by virtue of the space it takes in scholarly debate, the power of its agenda setting, and the lack of non-English sources — conceptual or otherwise — it tends to more or less inadvertently omit. I try, where possible, to introduce international literature from Francophone, Lusophone, German, and other scholarly and legal traditions, or cases situated in Brazil and the Netherlands, to reflect histories of speech regulation bound by fairly different processes. An example is in [Chapter 9](#/chapter-9), which makes the point of "normative dislocation" given a specific process of re-democratisation in Brazil posterior following its military regime. 

#### Transition

With these orientations in place, I turn first to the conceptual question on which the rest depends. Before one can study how moderation constitutes platforms, fractures into crises, or reorganises the geography of online speech, one needs a working account of what moderation *is*, and of how it differs from — and overlaps with — the older practices of censorship and debate management it has come to resemble. That is the task of [Part I. What is moderation?](#/part-1).
