# Abstract

In Internet studies, content moderation is typically framed as a question of platform regulation and governance. Yet, the etymology and older media uses of the term give reason to treat moderation more broadly, as one among several speech governance techniques — alongside censorship, gatekeeping, editorial convention and, latterly, AI alignment — through which public discourse is managed in relation to historically derived norms. The thesis asks: how does moderation govern public speech when the norms it materialises are contested? It approaches this question through five related problems: what kind of speech governance technique moderation is; how it becomes constitutive of platforms and their speech cultures; how disagreements over speech norms generate crises of legitimacy; how such crises reorganise public spheres into counter-spheres; and how moderation strategies evolve in response.

Drawing on digital methods — particularly trace research, which reconstructs what has been removed, demoted or relabelled from the residue it leaves behind — I argue that moderation is not the property of platforms alone but a modular force that materialises the boundaries of public discourse. It combines the censorial restraint of speech with the dialogical management of exchange, while its techniques and normative criteria can be recombined across platforms, publics and, increasingly, large language models. This *normative plasticity* explains both how less permitted speech migrates to wherever it is best expressed and why moderation repeatedly becomes an object of dispute in its own right. [Part I. What is moderation?](#/part-1) establishes this conceptual and methodological ground. [Part II. Speech affordances](#/part-2) shows how speech norms materialise as speech affordances. [Part III. Moderation crises](#/part-3) traces crises as norms diverge between platforms, users and other institutions. [Part IV. Ruptures](#/part-4) follows the counter-spheres, rival epistemic projects and folk theories these crises produce. [Part V. Modulation and consensus](#/part-5) traces a shift from adjudicative moderation towards modular, outsourced and consensus-building forms. The conclusion develops the implications of this account for moderation research, regulation and the design of public-interest infrastructures for disagreement.
