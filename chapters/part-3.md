# Part III. Moderation crises

[Part II. Speech affordances](#/part-2) examined how speech norms become material features of platforms: norms that take form in community guidelines, moderation techniques, interface functions and user cultures which signal what may be said, by whom and under what conditions. I called these arrangements *speech affordances*, i.e., the material possibilities for expression that arise from a speaker’s position, the capacities of a medium and the norms governing both [@ayalaSpeechAffordancesStructural2016; @bucherAffordancesSocialMedia2018]. Their role is constitutive, in the sense that they shape how speech moves within and between platforms.

Ironically, their constitutive role can be the source of significant contention. When there is no space for *metamoderation* — in which users may can discuss the terms through which moderation takes place in the form of appeals, public consultations and collective procedures [@geigerBotbasedCollectiveBlocklists2016b, 20; @seeringModeratorEngagementCommunity2019] — moderation is primarily relegated to its *enframing* function, i.e., the role of enforcing a particular norm. Thus emerges a *moderation crisis*: a situation in which conflict forms around the norms that speech affordances operationalise. This is especially relevant when these norms are based around essentially contested concepts. Racism, antisemitism, misinformation, incitement and militarism may be concepts whose ordinary use generates competing definitions [@gallieIXEssentiallyContestedConcepts1956; @delaatCoercionEmpowermentModeration2012]. This contestation is not in itself pathological but part of the “discursive contestation” through which publics form and revise normative standards [@fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990]. Crisis begins when the parties no longer share the premises through which the disagreement might be worked through, which Fogelin calls a *deep disagreement* [@fogelinLogicDeepDisagreements1985].

Because speech norms are materialised as affordances, this conflict also has material effects. At its limit, a normative boundary can make dialogue inviable not only because an utterance is prohibited, but because the terms needed to explain why it should or should not be prohibited no longer have a common place. As seen in [Part IV. Ruptures](#/part-4), a crisis then becomes a *rupture*, which occurs when actors no longer inhabit the same threshold of tolerance and begin to leave, exclude one another or build elsewhere. The disputed content does not necessarily disappear but becomes part of the normative and technical basis of an alternative space.

Most literature has tended to approach these instabilities from a different direction. A popular account treats online conflict as the result of insufficient moderation: the idea that the relaxation of editorial gatekeeping exposes publics to hostile actors, language and forms of informational manipulation that make communicative spaces uninhabitable. A related literature describes a wider field of “information disorders” in which hate speech, misinformation and coordinated influence overwhelm the capacity of platforms and publics to maintain informational integrity [@socialscienceresearchcouncilHateSpeechInformation2020]. Another version places responsibility on companies that respond slowly, inconsistently or only after public and commercial pressure. Moderation systems are then read through negligence: firms have the technical capacity to intervene but fail to do so because trust and safety remains subordinate to growth, engagement, compliance or brand protection [@gillespietarletonCustodiansInternet2018; @hillDefinitelyNotBusiness2024]. 

The four chapters in this part examine this process across different relations: between users and a platform in [Chapter 6](#/chapter-6); between social media audiences and editorial media in [Chapter 7](#/chapter-7); between a platform and the public authorities it is expected to follow in [Chapter 8](#/chapter-8); and between global platform policies and a local process of historical accountability in [Chapter 9](#/chapter-9). Together, they show how disagreement over content becomes disagreement over the norms, media and authorities through which public speech is governed.

[Chapter 6. Moderation in crisis](#/chapter-6) begins with a crisis between YouTube and its users. It compares discussions of objectionable information across 102 million comments posted between 2006 and 2022 with the platform’s changing policy architecture. Both are in motion, but not at the same rhythm. YouTube’s initially broad prohibitions become more detailed after 2014, separating hate speech, misinformation, harmful content and election integrity while moving from removal towards more dynamic forms of demotion. Commenters, meanwhile, continue to contest many of the categories that the platform must treat as settled enough to enforce.

The comparison distinguishes relatively sedimented norms from more variable ones. Murder, terrorism and child abuse are repeatedly condemned with little need for justification. Racism, antisemitism, discrimination, hate speech and misinformation remain subject to competing definitions. This is their essentially contested character: disagreement is not external to the concept but part of its use [@gallieIXEssentiallyContestedConcepts1956; @delaatCoercionEmpowermentModeration2012]. Over time, commenters move from discussing unacceptable acts, to unacceptable speech, and then to unacceptable ways of moderating speech. Censorship, deplatforming, shadowbanning and platform authority become objects of controversy in their own right.

The crisis lies in the distance between YouTube’s operational need to decide and the public process through which the categories behind those decisions acquire legitimacy. When that distance becomes a deep disagreement, users and the platform no longer contest only where a line should be drawn. They contest the premises by which a line could be recognised at all [@fogelinLogicDeepDisagreements1985]. This is one route from crisis to rupture. A platform rupture does not follow from every disputed decision. It occurs when users and a platform can no longer sustain the same threshold of tolerance, and exclusion or departure becomes the basis for another space with different speech affordances. Chapter 6 identifies the normative point at which such a movement becomes possible.

[Chapter 7. Of migrants and strangers](#/chapter-7) turns to a crisis between media types. It compares Dutch television coverage of the European refugee crisis with the responses of its Facebook and Twitter audiences between 2013 and 2018. The divergence is not simply one of political opinion. Broadcast news and social media organise speech through different editorial conventions, vocabularies and expectations of participation. Television coverage remains comparatively descriptive, attending to institutions, routes, countries and political responses. Sections of its social media audience increasingly speak of migration through the language of invasion, cultural threat and criminality.

The conflict reaches an apex after the sexual assaults in Cologne on New Year’s Eve 2015. Some German news organisations hesitated to identify the ethnic background of suspected perpetrators because the national press code restricted the singling out of ethnicity where it might reproduce discrimination. Many social media users interpreted this restraint, and the delay in coverage, as concealment. The controversy therefore concerned more than whether the assaults had taken place. It concerned how they could be reported: whether naming the perpetrators’ perceived origins was necessary for factual transparency or an illegitimate racialisation of crime.

This disagreement widened the distance between social media audiences and mainstream journalism. Users accused news media of lying, omitting controversial facts or enforcing “politically correct” language. They began to complement broadcast coverage with populist and radical right-wing sources such as Breitbart and GeenStijl, whose editorial styles permitted both more explicit reporting and more transgressive language about migrants and refugees. The result is a cross-media rupture. Users continued to respond to mainstream reporting, but increasingly relied on alternative media for the terms and narratives that established journalism did not afford. The crisis was consequently neither solely epistemic nor solely political. It formed around the speech norms through which an event could become news in the first place.

[Chapter 8. Twitter as an accidental authority](#/chapter-8) examines a crisis of epistemic authority during the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic. Platforms attempted to contain misinformation by directing users towards “authoritative sources” on transmission, treatment and prevention. Twitter, in particular, centralised access to public-health guidance, raised authoritative information and labelled claims that tended to contradict it. This strategy depended on those authorities providing a sufficiently coherent account of what was known. The WHO, CDC, NIH, PAHO and the White House, however, changed their guidance over time and contradicted one another on masks, hydroxychloroquine and modes of transmission, leaving their audiences divided around those contradictions. 

Twitter’s policy nevertheless required a boundary between trustworthy and misleading information, thus creating a paradox: it had organised moderation around deference to authority, but could no longer defer when authorities contradicted one another. In this situation, it responded by acting upon the degree of consensus among them, flagging or sanctioning outliers rather than establishing the objective truth of each claim (Donald Trump was the case in point). This decision-making placed Twitter in a situation once described by Carl Schmitt in *The Concept of the Political* [@schmittConceptPolitical1996]: that the sovereign is that who takes action in a state of exception. The state of exception, here, is one in which public (health) authorities — particularly those of most power (e.g. the White House) — lack consensus on COVID governance guidelines. Twitter steps in as an authority in its own right, even if just an accidental one.

[Chapter 9. Normative dislocation](#/chapter-9) is the culminating case of a crisis between Brazilian institutional actors and the moderation systems of US-based platforms around the 2022 presidential election and the attacks on the Three Powers Plaza on 8 January 2023. At the time, Brazil was undergoing a string of institutional crises when then-President Bolsonaro — in addition to political forces around him — aligned heavily on the idea of a military intervention in case of supposed electoral fraud. Such propositions were accompanied by a surge and relative normalisation of militaristic content online, which consisted in large part of advancing revisionist narratives about the role and actions of the Brazilian army during the military dictatorship of 1964-1980. This form of content — which in the US and Europe would equate in kind to WWII revisionist accounts — was treated by platforms as "electoral misinformation" developed in the context of the 2020 US election. 

This mismatch interrupted a longer local process of accountability. Since the end of military rule, amnesty, reparation, truth commissions, constitutional law and public memory have constituted an attempt to turn contested experiences of the military regime into a national experience of transitional justice and reconciliation. In their own way, these efforts consisted of moderation mechanisms as the management and facilitation of public debate ([Edwards, 2002](https://emxpert.net/sageedit/journals/Embox/Index/1364814#bibr16)), particularly by establishing a common vocabulary of human rights, the documentation of political violence and renegotiating the place of the armed forces in public life. Such processes were never complete, but they supplied a historical basis from which calls for a new military intervention could be understood as infringements against a collective memory.

Marcelo Alves dos Santos Junior and I call the platform mismatch a *normative dislocation*: a situation whereby moderation system based upon foreign norms ceases to participate in a local process of norm formation. By treating militaristic speech primarily as electoral misinformation, or by failing to recognise it as an object of moderation, platforms contradicted and partially derouted the Brazilian process of accountability. As such, the affordances of global platforms allowed a local history of political violence to return under norms that could not properly name it. 

The chapter concludes with the need to connect platform moderation to local forms of metamoderation. Participative policy development, involving civil society, legislators, historians and affected publics, would place platform rules in relation to the histories they are meant to govern. Context and provenance mechanisms, for example, could make the premises behind disputed historical claims more visible. Naturally, such measures would not be sufficient to settle Brazil’s conflict over military rule, but they would provide a space in which the relevance of that history to (and as) moderation could be discussed before enforcement becomes the only available interface between platforms and public institutions.

Across these four cases, moderation becomes a crisis when the norms materialised by speech affordances lose a common space of discussion. Chapter 6 locates this crisis between a platform and its users; Chapter 7 between editorial media and social media audiences; Chapter 8 between a platform and the authorities it was expected to follow; and Chapter 9 between global platform policies and a local history of political accountability. The actors and objects differ, but the movement is the same. Disagreement about content becomes disagreement about the terms of moderation, then about the authority entitled to enforce them.

These cases do not show that every moderation crisis can be resolved through consensus, but rather why the absence of metamoderation matters. Where actors retain a space to clarify definitions, supply histories, contest procedures and revise rules, disagreement may remain part of moderation. Where no such space exists, the affordances of one medium become intolerable to some of those who inhabit it. [Part IV. Ruptures](#/part-4) follows what these movements create.
